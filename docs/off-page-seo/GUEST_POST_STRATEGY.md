## Rules for every pitch

- One article, one publication. Do not syndicate the same draft across a guest-post network.
- The host sets the anchor. Ask for a brand or partial-match anchor if they ask you to choose. Decline an exact-match anchor that says "best free YouTube downloader" or any phrase that promises HD, unlimited saves, or private-account access.
- One or two contextual links are enough. A third link is only for a primary source such as a platform help page or a copyright statute the editor already trusts.
- Disclose that the writer is associated with ReelFetch when the bio mentions it.
- Do not add a keyword block, a comparison table that invents scores, or a line that tells readers to ignore a platform's terms.

## Draft 1 — Before you save a public video

Suggested title: Before you save a public video, answer four questions  
Alternative title: A short permission checklist for Reels, Shorts, and public posts  
Intended publications: digital-literacy blogs, library or classroom-tech newsletters, creator newsletters  
Meta description for the editor, not for keyword stuffing: Four questions to answer before saving a public Reel, Short, or video, plus what to do when the answer is no.

### Article

A public link is not the same thing as permission to keep the file.

That distinction is easy to miss. The video plays in a browser. The share button hands over a URL. A dozen websites offer to turn that URL into an MP4. None of those facts tell you whether you are allowed to store the copy, show it in class, or post it somewhere else.

Before you save a public video, four questions are enough.

**Did you make it, or do you have a clear yes from the person who did?**

The comfortable case is your own upload. You posted the Reel, the Short, or the clip, and you want a file on your computer because apps bury their archive tools. Saving your own work is the use that needs the least debate.

The uncomfortable case is someone else's work that happens to be public. "Public" means you can watch it without a password. It does not mean the creator donated the copyright. A newsroom, a teacher, and a fan edit do not get the same answer. If you cannot explain the use in a sentence a rights holder would accept, do not download the file.

**Is the post actually public?**

Many "it will not download" moments are the tool doing the right thing. Private accounts, friends-only posts, deleted videos, and clips limited to one country are not yours to pull through a third-party site. A downloader that asks you to log in as someone else, or that claims to open private profiles, is asking you to cross a line that the platforms draw on purpose.

If the link is public and it is your own, paste that exact URL. A screenshot of the caption is not a video link.

**What will you do with the file after it is on disk?**

Offline viewing of something you were allowed to save is different from uploading it to another channel, cutting it into an ad, or stripping a watermark and presenting the clip as yours. Watermarks are not a technical nuisance standing between you and a free asset. They are often how a creator or a platform marks the copy.

Write down the use before you fetch the file. "I need a backup of the Short I published last week" is a clear purpose. "I might use it later" is how files end up in a shared drive nobody can justify.

**Which copy are you actually getting?**

Web tools do not invent resolution. They can only offer a format the source returns for that URL. A page that promises Full HD on every link is advertising a decision it does not control. If the result lists 720p and not 1080p, the higher file was not offered. Saving twice will not create it.

The same limit applies to audio. Some hosts store picture and sound separately. A combined file exists only when the tool can build one from what the host sent. If sound is missing, the honest next step is to try the original post, not a chain of mirror sites.

### A practical way to check a public link

When the four answers are satisfactory, the mechanics are ordinary. Copy the URL from the public post. Use a site that states its limits in normal language. Pick a quality that appears in the result. Let the browser save the file to its usual downloads folder.

[ReelFetch](https://bestdownloader.site/) is one site built around that narrower job. It has separate notes for [YouTube](https://bestdownloader.site/platform/youtube), [Instagram](https://bestdownloader.site/platform/instagram), [TikTok](https://bestdownloader.site/platform/tiktok), [Facebook](https://bestdownloader.site/platform/facebook), X, Reddit, and Pinterest. Those pages are worth reading because they say what will not be fetched, including private accounts and posts the source will not return. The [copyright page](https://bestdownloader.site/copyright) tells rights holders how to send a notice. ReelFetch is not affiliated with those platforms.

If you operate a different tool, the checklist does not change. Any service that needs your password, that claims to download private media, or that hides the download behind a fake play button is a poor place to paste a URL.

### If the answer to any question is no

Leave the file where it is. Link to the public post instead of embedding a copy you cannot explain. If you are a rights holder and a site is distributing your work, use that site's published contact rather than a random social reply. A useful notice includes the URLs, proof you own the work, and a statement that you are the owner or the owner's agent.

Saving a video is a small technical act. Keeping it is a decision. The checklist is there so the technical act does not happen first.

## Draft 2 — What a browser does when it saves a video file

Suggested title: What actually happens when your browser saves a video  
Alternative title: A plain-language tour of a video download  
Intended publications: general technology explainers, browser or web-performance blogs, digital-literacy columns  
Meta description for the editor: A non-technical account of how a video file moves from a public page to a downloads folder, and which parts a website cannot control.

### Article

The downloads folder makes a video look like a single object. The trip it took to get there is a series of ordinary web requests.

You start with a page address. That address identifies a post, not always a file. A YouTube Short, an Instagram Reel, a TikTok clip, and a Reddit video are pages that know how to play media. The file you want may be one response or several: picture in one stream, sound in another, and a player that joins them while you watch.

When you use the video host's own button, the host decides whether a file is part of the product. Some hosts provide an official save or export for the account that uploaded the video. Some do not. A third-party website cannot overrule that design. It can only ask, with the URL you supplied, whether a public media file is available to a logged-out viewer.

### The request

A careful tool checks the URL before it does anything else. Is it a complete `https://` link? Does the host match a service the tool knows how to ask? A link that merely looks like a video — a photo pin, a text post, a private profile — should end as an explanation, not as a broken file.

If the URL is plausible, the tool asks the source for formats. The answer might be a list of sizes. It might be an error because the post was deleted, limited to one country, or never public. The useful interface shows that result in words. A spinner that ends in a disguised advertisement is not a format list.

### The file

If a format is available, your browser saves bytes to the place it always uses for downloads. On a phone, that place might be the browser's download list rather than a gallery. Nothing about a website forces iOS or Android to file the clip beside your camera photos.

Progress on screen is the browser receiving the response. It is not a promise about how long the source will take, and it is not a promise that the file will be 1080p. The source either offered that size or it did not.

Some services advertise that they delete temporary copies after the transfer. That claim is about the tool's own disks. It does not change copyright in the file now sitting in your downloads folder. You still have to be able to explain why you kept it.

### What a website should refuse

A browser cannot ethically be pointed at media you are not allowed to request. Private accounts, paid videos, and login-only posts are the obvious cases. So are pages that ask you to install an extension you did not intend to install, or to disable a security control so the download will "work."

It is also reasonable for a tool to refuse to invent a format. Merging separate picture and sound is possible only when both parts arrive and the server or the device can combine them. If one part is missing, the honest result is a smaller set of options or an error.

[ReelFetch](https://bestdownloader.site/) describes itself in those terms: paste a public link, review the qualities that source returns, and save a listed file. Its [FAQ](https://bestdownloader.site/faq) states that private and region-blocked videos are not downloaded, and that Full HD is included only when the source offers it. That is the standard a downloader page should be willing to print, whatever brand is on it.

### How to tell a careful page from a careless one

Read the page before you paste. You want a named operator, a contact address, and a takedown process. You want the text to admit limits. You do not want a guarantee of every website, every quality, and every private account.

Then look at the result, not the advertisement. If the only button that looks like "download" is an ad, close the tab. If the qualities listed are specific to your link, you are looking at a real response. Pick one and let the browser finish.

The file on disk is the end of the technical story and the start of the editorial one. Name the creator, keep the context, and do not upload the copy anywhere your answers from the permission checklist would not cover.
